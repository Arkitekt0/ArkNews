How La Junta’s Historical Sites Support the Community

By Ronald Hernandez Jr. | August 16, 2026 | Local News

Economic Impact on La Junta

La Junta’s historic sites generate documented economic and cultural benefits for southeastern Colorado, supported by local data from the National Park Service. The agency reports that visitors to Bent’s Old Fort National Historic Site spent 1.3 million dollars in nearby communities in 2022, supported 17 local jobs, and contributed 1.7 million dollars in total economic output, as detailed in the National Park Service Economic Benefit Report.

Bent's Old Fort: A National Landmark Driving Tourism

Bent’s Old Fort also serves as a major historic landmark along the Santa Fe Trail. History Colorado documents that the site functioned as a central trading location between 1821 and 1880, connecting travelers, Indigenous nations, military expeditions, and commercial networks across the plains. The organization notes that the fort continues to attract visitors seeking information about early commerce and frontier life.

FOX Theater: A Century-old Beacon of La Junta

The FOX Theater contributes additional cultural value to La Junta’s historic district. Cinema Treasures records the building opening in 1912 as the Rourke Opera House and later operating under FOX Intermountain Theaters beginning in 1939. The venue was renovated into two screening rooms with 159 and 96 seats and reopened in 2001. Colorado Office of Economic Development and International Trade reports that the theater contributes to downtown revitalization through the Reimagine Destinations program. The Colorado Community Revitalization Grant program identifies historic mixed‑use cultural venues as qualifying projects for redevelopment funding.

Otero Museum's Role in Preserving Local History

The Otero Museum preserves regional records related to railroads, agriculture, military service, and early settlement. A museum guide stated, “Our warehouses are full of stuff with nowhere to put it. That shows how much history this town has and how important it is to save artifacts.”

Otero Community College's Role in Regional Education

Otero Junior College opened in 1941 and expanded workforce training, academic programs, and athletics throughout the twentieth century.

Connecting History, Agriculture, and Community Identity

Visitors are encouraged to explore these historic sites and experience the cultural heritage that continues to influence La Junta.

Colorado’s Historic Milestone: A Century and a Half in the Union

By Ronald Hernandez Jr. | August 1, 2026 | Local News
Image provided by: History Colorado
This image shows an issue of the La Junta Tribune Democrat from January 1, 1898.

On this day in history, Colorado marks 150 years since its admission to the United States, a milestone recorded on August 1, 1876, when President Ulysses S. Grant signed the proclamation officially admitting Colorado as the 38th state in the Union, as documented by the Library of Congress.

The region’s history stretches far earlier than its statehood. Early inhabitants included agricultural cliff‑dwelling communities, followed centuries later by Spanish expeditions such as Francisco Vásquez de Coronado’s 1540s exploration. Interest in the region intensified in the late 1850s when gold discoveries near Central City drew thousands of prospectors, according to historical summaries from History.com.

Mining camps and ranching communities became central to early territorial life. Historical accounts compiled by the Aspen Times note that miners and ranchers shaped Colorado’s early economy, influenced settlement patterns, and contributed to the territorial push toward organized governance.

Original Coloradans

Research from the Library of Congress and documentary work by PBS identify Colorado’s earliest known inhabitants as Indigenous nations including the Ute, Cheyenne, Arapaho, and Apache peoples. These communities developed cultural traditions, trade networks, and seasonal migration patterns long before European or American settlement.

PBS documentation highlights that these groups were the region’s original Coloradans, forming the foundation of Colorado’s earliest human history and shaping cultural and environmental practices across the plains, mountains, and river valleys.

Early American Settlement

According to early settlement records preserved by Grand County, American migration into Colorado increased significantly during the mid‑1800s. Settlers established homesteads, ranches, and agricultural communities across mountain valleys and plains regions, often near water sources, transportation routes, and mining districts.

Local history guides from the Library of Congress note that early settlement patterns were shaped by geography, available resources, and the expansion of mining and ranching industries. These communities contributed to the territorial population growth that later supported Colorado’s bid for statehood.

Political organization began with the establishment of the Colorado Territory in 1861. Several proposed constitutions were rejected, and Congress declined earlier admission attempts. The 1875 enabling act finally authorized Colorado to draft a constitution meeting federal requirements, including taxation rules, water rights provisions, and limits on state borrowing, as outlined in EBSCO Research Starters.

Delegates convened in late 1875 for the constitutional convention, working through early 1876 to produce the document required for admission. Their work coincided with national celebrations approaching the centennial of the Declaration of Independence.

Voters approved the new constitution on July 1, 1876. Reports describe crowds filling the streets on July 4 as thirty‑eight ceremonial shots were fired to symbolize Colorado’s anticipated place as the thirty‑eighth state.

On August 1, 1876, President Grant issued the proclamation admitting Colorado to the Union. The date has since remained a significant marker in Colorado’s history, commemorated annually as Colorado Day.

Early Settlers Day – La Junta

In La Junta, Early Settlers Day remains one of the community’s longest‑running traditions. According to event organizers and historical descriptions from Colorado.com, the celebration takes place annually in City Park and features historic demonstrations, vendors, food booths, games, and family‑friendly activities that highlight pioneer heritage.

Photo credit: Vanessa Photography

The celebration connects local history with Colorado’s broader territorial story, including the miners, ranchers, Indigenous communities, and agricultural families who contributed to the development of southeastern Colorado.

As Colorado observes its sesquicentennial, communities across the state continue to highlight the events, traditions, and historical developments that shaped its entry into the Union. Early Settlers Day serves as a reminder of the stories preserved in towns like La Junta.
