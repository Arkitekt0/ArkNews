Understanding El Niño’s Influence on Southeastern Colorado

By Ronald Hernandez Jr.

Climate patterns that impact southeastern Colorado often begin thousands of miles away in the tropical Pacific Ocean. During a recent interview for The Blueprint, climatologist Allie Mazurek of the Colorado Climate Center explained how El Niño events develop, how scientists identify them, and why they matter for communities across Otero County. Her explanations provide important context for residents who have observed shifting weather trends in recent years.

El Niño is one phase of the broader El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO), a climate pattern defined by changes in sea surface temperatures in the equatorial Pacific. According to Mazurek, El Niño occurs when ocean temperatures rise above normal, which alters atmospheric circulation and shifts storm tracks across the United States. The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) provides detailed monitoring and ENSO outlooks for these temperature changes and their global effects.

These changes can influence precipitation in Colorado, particularly in the southern part of the state. Mazurek noted that fall months show the strongest historical connection between El Niño conditions and above-average moisture. Winter outcomes are more complex, with northern Colorado sometimes experiencing increased snowfall during La Niña years. She emphasized that these patterns are not absolute, citing recent winters in which expected results did not occur. The Colorado Climate Center also describes ENSO’s Colorado influence as variable, and NOAA presents seasonal outlooks as probabilities rather than fixed predictions.

Advances in climate modeling and machine learning are improving long-range forecasting, although Mazurek described seasonal prediction as an area that continues to develop. Global models can identify broad trends, but local outcomes — such as whether a storm will track directly over Otero County — remain difficult to determine months in advance.

Mazurek also highlighted ways communities can support climate research. The Community Collaborative Rain, Hail and Snow Network (CoCoRaHS) invites volunteers to report daily precipitation, providing observations used by weather and climate professionals.

Her insights show how global climate systems influence local conditions and how community participation strengthens scientific understanding across southeastern Colorado.
